# GM Notes — The Willow That Came Back
### First-Time Storyguide's Running Guide

---

## Before You Start: The Two Things That Make This Work

**1. The magi caused everything bad in this session, but none of it is their fault as a choice.**
Aldric drained the marsh because it was the right thing to do for the covenant. Corvus manipulated
Reeve Eckard because it was the smart play. Konstantinos buried the flask because he needed the
measurement. Each act was reasonable. Each act had consequences they did not fully anticipate, or
knew and underweighted, or knew and said nothing about. This is the heart of the covenant
concept: the schemes work, and the side effects are the story.

**2. The grogs are insiders, not bystanders.**
They are not innocent peasants who stumbled into wizard problems. They are sworn covenant soldiers
who know approximately what their masters do and have chosen to serve anyway. Their loyalty is
real; their fear is real; and their frustration at never being told the full story is also real.
Play all three. The grogs can be angry at Corvus for the cryptic briefing and still carry out his
orders. That tension is the flavor.

---

## What Each Scene Teaches and Why

### Scene 1 — The Courtyard at Dawn
**Teaching goal:** The character sheet.

This scene has no dice. Its job is orientation — what is a Characteristic, what is an Ability,
what is a Personality Trait — delivered through in-character introductions rather than a rules
lecture. Go around the table and ask each player to say one thing their grog is thinking as they
wait in the cold courtyard.

Corvus's brief appearance sets the tone for the entire session: the magi give orders, do not
explain, and go back inside. This is not cruelty (entirely) — it is the social reality of
Mythic Europe's covenant structure. Magi live in laboratories and think in terms of seasons.
Grogs live in the world and think in terms of days. They are not peers.

**Pacing note:** Keep this short. Five minutes of introductions, one minute of Corvus, done.
Do not let it become a planning session — there is nothing to plan. They know where the flask is.

**If players ask about the Latin note in the satchel:** They cannot read it (nobody in the grog
group has Latin). Brecht can tell them it is probably important. It says "Do not approach the
water. There may not be water. This is still the instruction." This is characteristically
Konstantinos — precise about conditions that seem contradictory, zero interest in explaining
himself. They can try to find someone to translate. There is nobody at the covenant gate who will
help them; the covenant's servants scatter from magi-related errands. Let this unease sit.

---

### Scene 2 — The Meadow Path
**Teaching goal:** The Simple Die and Ease Factors.

This scene is deliberately low-stakes. The meadow is beautiful and slightly wrong, and the goal
is to give players their first rolls in a context where failure has no bite — a wet boot, a laugh,
a hand offered to pull someone out of the peat. Let everyone roll something. Let someone fail
extravagantly (Gervas trying to leap the slump at full speed and going face-first into black mud
is genuinely funny and will be remembered).

**On the flask itself:** Make the retrieval easy. Ease Factor 3. The flask comes free cleanly.
It is warm. It pulses faintly — not a heartbeat, more like a sleeping breath, once every twenty
seconds. Describe this matter-of-factly, as if the grogs have handled worse. They probably have.
What the flask contains is raw vis from the regio threshold; this is irrelevant to the grogs and
need not be explained.

**The ground seeping back:** Liesel's Awareness roll (or whoever notices) is the first indication
that the marsh is reasserting itself. Do not underline it. Mention it once. Let it accumulate in
the players' minds: the drainage is not holding at the south end. This will be visible at Scene 5.

**The willow:** Let the players look at it. Describe it neutrally — large, healthy, root-circle
has broken the surface, grass inside the roots is the wrong color of green. Do not add menace yet.
The menace should arrive naturally by Scene 5. Right now it is just a tree that should not exist.

---

### Scene 3 — The Assayer's Men
**Teaching goal:** The Stress Die and the botch.

This is the session's social and mechanical pivot. Albrecht is lying and the grogs know something
is wrong — but the document looks real, and his tone is entirely reasonable, and Heinrich and
Kaspar are resting their hands near their belts. There is social pressure. That means Stress Dice.

Walk through the mechanics aloud the first time a grog rolls:
> *"There's pressure here — social stakes, those two watching — so this is a Stress Die, not a
> Simple Die. Roll d10. If you get a 1: great, reroll and double it, and double again if you
> get another 1 — spectacular reads do happen. If you get a 0: hold on. That might be a botch.
> Roll one botch die. If that also shows 0: something goes wrong."*

**On the botch:** A botch here means the grog misreads Albrecht and accepts the document as
legitimate. This is not a disaster — it just means the grogs have now (accidentally) given a
fraudulent surveyor tacit covenant permission to operate on the meadow boundary. Corvus will be
displeased. More importantly, it means the situation is not neatly resolved, and the players
feel the mechanical shape of an unresolved complication.

**What Albrecht actually wants:** Access to the ridge survey, not a fight. If the grogs produce
Brecht's Leadership and move past him civilly, he will go up the ridge with Heinrich and Kaspar
and file his assay later. This is still a problem, but it is a later problem — for Corvus, not
for the grogs tonight. You can tell the players this flatly: "Your orders don't include stopping
a surveyor. Your orders are to get the flask back before Vespers."

**If a player wants Tomas to handle the social scene:** He has a stammer and Com −4. Let him try.
Let it go badly in a funny way. This is the lesson — different abilities solve different problems,
and "best fighter" does not translate to "best talker."

**Pacing note:** Do not let this scene drag. Ten to fifteen minutes at most. Albrecht is an
obstacle to think around, not a puzzle to fully solve. The point is the mechanics and the political
texture; the claim-jumpers are the actual fight.

---

### Scene 4 — The Ambush at the Culvert
**Teaching goal:** Full combat.

Run the whole sequence once, slowly, narrating as you go. After the first round, it will speed up.

**Staging matters:** The culvert is a four-foot drop onto stone at the path's edge. Position one
claim-jumper near it. If the Berserker charges that one recklessly (he will), there is a chance
the claim-jumper is knocked into the culvert — or the Berserker is. A botch on Defense near the
culvert: the grog slips on the wet stone and goes down one Fatigue level and loses their next
action. This is cinematic, not lethal. Use it freely.

**On grog death:** A grog can die here if they take a very bad wound and the Storyguide rules it
fatal. This is allowed, even on a first session. The genre does not protect named characters.
If Gervas takes an Incapacitating wound, he is unconscious; if the damage total was high enough,
it may be fatal — make the call based on the number, not sentiment. Mourn him briefly and
move on. The other grogs carry him back. Aldric will be mildly inconvenienced.

**Claim-jumper morale:** They are not soldiers. When the first one goes down, have the others
roll Brave (trait only, treat as a die roll vs. Ease Factor 6). One likely flees immediately.
If the second goes down — or Gervas makes a charge that looks genuinely terrifying — the third
runs. End the fight on a high point, not a cleanup.

**After the fight:** The grogs are late, one may be injured, and the gate is visible from here.
Let them feel the relief of proximity. Then have Liesel look back toward the south meadow.

---

### Scene 5 — What Grows in the Dark
**Teaching goal:** The world is bigger than them.

This scene has no rolls. It is pure atmosphere and worldbuilding, and it should be short.

**Aldric's presence:** Aldric does not try to frighten the grogs. He is not theatrical. He is
simply — there, in the gateway, in the early dark, looking past them at the willow. His Blatant
Gift means the grogs physically cannot look at him comfortably. Play this as a social texture note,
not a mechanical one: nobody makes eye contact. People find things to look at. This is normal.
They have all worked for Aldric for years and it never gets easier.

His two lines of dialogue are everything the grogs need to know. Do not add to them. Do not let
a player argue with him through the gate — the gate is closed, and Aldric is gone.

**The pulsing light:** Liesel should roll for this, or any high-Per grog who looks back.
The light is cold and rhythmic and absolutely should not be there. It is not immediately
dangerous tonight. It will be soon. This is a hook, not a threat. The grogs correctly
identify that this is not their department and go to bed.

**Konstantinos's light in the tower:** Add this detail as a final image before you close the
session. The scrying station is active. He has been listening all day. He knows exactly what
happened — the soft ground, Albrecht, the claim-jumpers, the flask. He said nothing to warn them.
He was observing. This is, for Konstantinos, entirely normal scholarly behavior.

## Pacing — The Session as a Whole

| Scene | Rough Time | Die Introduced |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Courtyard | 10–15 min | None — just the sheet |
| 2 — Meadow Path | 20–25 min | Simple Die |
| 3 — Assayer | 20–25 min | Stress Die + botch |
| 4 — Ambush | 40–50 min | Full combat |
| 5 — Willow at Dusk | 10–15 min | None — pure atmosphere |
| **Total** | **~2.5–3 hrs** | |

If you are running long: trim Scene 3 (have Albrecht be easy to deflect, skip the botch mechanics
and just describe the Stress Die exploding for someone). If you are running short: add an extra
exchange in Scene 3 (have Albrecht request to accompany them back to the gate, which means the
claim-jumpers appear with an audience) or let the combat go an extra round.

## Handling Failure

**Scene 2 failure (wet boots, slumped ground):** Fully embrace it. Let the Berserker be
genuinely stuck in peat to the knee while Liesel says "I said this would happen." No rolls to
escape — just Wulf grabbing his arm and pulling. This is comedy, not drama.

**Scene 3 botch (accepted the document):** The grogs have not done anything wrong by covenant
law — they do not have authority to deny surveyors. But Corvus will find out through
Konstantinos's listening web, and he will be displeased, and the grogs will feel this later.
You do not need to resolve this tonight. Plant it and move on.

**Scene 3 botch (accused Albrecht of fraud publicly):** Now Albrecht is insulted and Heinrich
draws his sword. This is a social crisis, not a combat — Brecht's Leadership can defuse it, or
the grogs can simply leave quickly. Albrecht will remember, and file his report to the guild with
a note about the covenant's "aggressive" servants. Again: later problem.

**Scene 4 grog death:** Handle it directly. Say "Gervas took [number] damage, which exceeds his
Incapacitated threshold — he's badly wounded and unconscious, and given the damage total I'm going
to rule he doesn't make it." Then: "How do you carry him?" The covenant pays for decent burial.
The magi will not be present at it.

**Scene 4 — grogs running:** If the players want to retreat from the claim-jumpers: fine. The
claim-jumpers want the satchel, not blood — if the grogs drop the satchel and run, the jumpers
take it and go. Now the grogs have to tell Corvus they lost the flask. This is worse, in many
ways, than a fight. Run this consequence honestly. Corvus will send them back with precise
instructions about where, exactly, to recover it from.

**Scene 5 — grogs trying to investigate the willow tonight:** Brecht should intervene. "The mage
said not to go near it after dark. That is a direct order." If they go anyway: nothing immediately
bad happens. The light pulses. The roots are warmer than they should be. One grog gets a
headache that will last until morning. Nothing more — but they should feel that something noticed
them. Liesel will note that she said so.

## Keeping the Magi Off-Stage but Felt

This is the hardest thing in the session and the most important.

**The rule:** the magi appear physically in exactly two moments — Corvus at the opening gate,
Aldric at the closing gate. That is all. Every other magi presence is environmental: the map in
the satchel, the listening-stone's carved surface at the well (don't point it out, let Liesel
notice it if she looks), the warm flask, the too-fast hedgerow, the tower light.

**What the grogs know and when:**
- They know Corvus sent them. They do not know why this particular flask is urgent.
- They know the marsh was drained. They do not know what Konstantinos measured there.
- They know Aldric is proud of the meadow. They do not know his Herbam spells are why the
  willow came back — the magic in the soil gave the dormant thing energy.
- They know Reeve Eckard is "cooperative." They do not know why; the smarter grogs may suspect.

**Do not explain any of this.** The grogs' partial knowledge is the texture of their world.
They are insiders who are kept precisely as informed as their masters judge sufficient. This is
not unusual to them. It is the shape of their lives.

**If players ask "why did the willow come back?":** Brecht shrugs. "Ask the mage." They could,
theoretically, ask the mage. Nobody wants to ask the mage. This is correct behavior.

**Konstantinos is listening through the whole session.** He knows about Albrecht, the
claim-jumpers, and the flask. He will not act. He is observing the regio's behavior through
the flask's resonance. If the grogs knew this, they would feel surveilled. They do not know.
They simply feel, in some nonspecific way, that the valley is not entirely private.

## The Code Tension — Setting Up the Saga

If players want to continue into a full saga, the session plants three seeds that connect to the
setting's central Code-of-Hermes tension:

**"Interfering with mundanes"** is the elastic clause that the magi's every act of visible
world-changing tickles. The Benedictine prior is writing letters. Albrecht's assay will arrive at
a guild office. Reeve Eckard is, without knowing it, behaving strangely in a way that eventually
someone will notice. The valley's abundance is conspicuous.

**The laundering problem:** The magi's task is not building the valley — they have done that.
Their task now is explaining it. A naturally excellent harvest is Providence. Three consecutive
unnaturally excellent harvests, with a drained marsh and an apparently spontaneous silver strike,
is theology. The bishop's secretary who studied canon law will want an explanation.

**The grogs' position:** When the Quaesitor eventually arrives to investigate the covenant's
interference with mundanes, the grogs will be interviewed. They know things — not the full
picture, but enough to be uncomfortable. Their loyalty to the covenant is real. Their ignorance
of the laws that govern magi is also real. They are caught exactly in the middle, which is
exactly where they have always been.

**The line to end the session on** (say this to the players, breaking the fiction for a moment):
> *"The covenant is doing everything it set out to do. The valley is richer, the silver is
> found, the reeve is compliant, and the meadow is beautiful. Every one of those things is
> going to be a problem. That's the show."*

Then go to the pub.

## A Note for Storyguides New to Ars Magica

The hardest habit to break coming from other RPGs is expecting the magi to rescue the grogs.
They won't. They are not watching to make sure the grogs are safe — they are watching to see
what happens to the flask. This is not villainy. It is a profoundly alien set of priorities
combined with the genuine conviction that the grogs are capable adults who can handle a walk
to a tree.

The grogs' competence — and occasional death — is not a failure state. It is the setting.
Ars Magica is a game about a community of people trying to live alongside beings whose power and
perspective is genuinely incomprehensible to them, while those beings build things that will last
for centuries. The grogs are the human scale. Honor that scale. Let them be brave and unlucky and
funny and sometimes dead. The magi will still be there when they are gone, thinking about something
else entirely.

That contrast — the grogs' brief, mortal, ground-level story against the magi's long, cold,
world-historical story — is the game. This session is the first page of it.
