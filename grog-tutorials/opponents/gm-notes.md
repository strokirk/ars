# Storyguide's Running Notes — *The Steward's Errand*

### For the First-Time Storyguide

This is your prep document. It is longer than the scenarios warrant because Ars Magica has some
structural assumptions that differ from other RPGs, and navigating them for the first time is
the hard part. Read this once before the session, keep it open during play, and trust the
pedagogy — it was designed so that if you run each scene in order, the rules teach themselves.

---

## The Central Constraint: Keep the Magi Off the Table

This is the hardest job you have tonight. The covenant's magi are your final boss and your best
reveal, and both of those things only work if you resist the temptation to use them earlier.

**Why this matters mechanically:** The grogs have no tools to interact with magi. No Parma
Magica, no magic resistance, no spells. A direct confrontation at any scene before Scene 5 will
either kill grogs instantly (too much, too fast, session over) or feel like the Storyguide pulling
punches (which it is). Neither teaches anything. The magic is the wall the grogs run into at the
end — it only works as a wall if they've been building momentum toward it.

**How to keep them off-stage:**
- The magi have no *need* to confront the grogs directly. They know who they are, when they
  arrived, and why. They are watching and waiting. This is in character — Corvus schemes, Konstantinos
  monitors, Aldric reclaims. None of them regards a steward's investigation as a crisis.
- Covenfolk and covenant grogs handle any actual friction. The magi will not come down from the
  tower because five soldiers are asking Dietrich questions.
- Aldric at the mill is the one exception, and even there he does nothing. He is present. The Gift
  does the work. He does not need to fight, threaten, or speak.
- Corvus appears in Scene 5, gives one sentence, and departs. That's the entire encounter.
- Konstantinos is never physically present. The players will know he exists only through his effects.

**The rule:** if a player asks "can we go to the tower?", the answer is not "no" — it's "the gate
is closed and the covenant's grogs are there; what do you do?" The players run into the mundane
wall first and have to decide how hard to push it.

## Scene-by-Scene Running Notes

### Scene 1 — The Muster at Baldewin's Gate
**What it teaches:** Character sheets. Nothing else.

**Your job:** Make Werner feel real and specific — he is a competent steward, not a blusterer.
He is sending soldiers on an administrative mission because Baldewin ordered it, and Werner is
professionally unhappy about that. He will ask for them back intact. Let his dry, slightly worried
authority set the tone: this is a real world where people have jobs and bosses.

Go around the table. Each player reads their grog's name, one Personality Trait, and best Ability.
Have Werner react to each one. This takes ten minutes. Do not skip it — it's the moment players
decide who their grog *is*, and that decision shapes the whole session.

**If players ask questions Werner can't answer:** he can't. He knows what's on his card. He doesn't
know who the tower's occupants are, whether they're dangerous, or what "scholars" in this context
means. His ignorance is real and it's a feature — the players are about to learn something Werner
doesn't know.

**Pacing note:** Don't drag this scene. Fifteen minutes maximum. It's a warm-up, not the show.

---

### Scene 2 — Rohrbeck
**What it teaches:** Simple Die and Ease Factors.

**Your job:** Make Dietrich specific and three-dimensional. He is not a villain. He is a small
man who has been given a very good deal and has not been asked too many questions about it, and he
has been living with the slight discomfort of that bargain for three years. The covenant has not
been unkind to him or to Rohrbeck. The village is genuinely better off. He believes this is God's
will, mostly because the alternative interpretation is deeply frightening.

**Running the Social Checks:**
- These are Simple Dice — no botch risk. Explain this explicitly the first time: *"You're not
  under pressure here, just asking questions. Roll a d10, add the listed number, tell me if you
  hit 9."*
- Let failures mean stonewalling, not disaster. Dietrich says "I cannot say" or "I don't know
  exactly" — information withheld, not confrontation triggered. Save confrontation for the Stress
  Die scene.
- Success on the Folk Ken (reading his fear): give the players something real — *"He's telling
  the truth. He's also scared. He deflects every question about the occupants' faces. He has
  seen something that he doesn't want to see again."* This is more useful than statistics.

**The grain detail:** Push the wrongness of the fields gently. If nobody rolls Awareness, describe
it anyway: *"You have all seen fields. You know what fields look like. These don't look like that."*
Let it sit.

**Pacing note:** This scene can run 20–30 minutes if the players are engaged. Let them ask Dietrich
questions. Their curiosity is the engine. Cut when they run out of questions or when they decide
to go see the mill.

---

### Scene 3 — The Mill
**What it teaches:** Stress Die and the botch.

**Your job:** Ilse's line — *"You took longer than expected, Sergeant Harbert"* — is the pivot
of the session. Deliver it flatly, matter-of-factly, as if she said something completely ordinary.
Let the players sit with it for a beat before they react. The eeriness is in the normalcy, not
the drama.

**Explaining the Stress Die:**
When the first Stress Die comes up, say this: *"Now there's something at stake. Same roll as
before, but: if you roll a 1, reroll and double — and keep doubling if 1s keep coming. If you
roll a 0, that's a botch check — roll another die; a second 0 means something goes really wrong.
This die tells us whether this moment is remembered forever or forgotten by morning."*

Then let someone roll. Make sure the first Stress Die happens before anyone processes it fully —
the explanation and the roll should happen fast. The die teaches itself.

**The botch on Folk Ken:** If it happens, run it: the grog misreads Ilse as a threat and
overreacts — grabs her arm, draws a weapon, shouts something accusatory. She screams (she's a
covenfolk servant, not a warrior). Harbert (the Sergeant) has about sixty seconds to talk the
situation down before Wendel and two other covenant grogs appear from behind the millhouse. This
is a Social Combat in miniature — Leadership or Folk Ken or Guile vs. Ease 9 to de-escalate. It's
chaotic and embarrassing and nobody gets hurt, and it is excellent teaching material.

**If the botch doesn't happen:** Still note the spiraling water. *"The mill-race runs straight, but
the water in it doesn't. It curls in slow shapes — not spirals exactly, more like writing you can't
read. It's been doing this all afternoon."* Don't explain it. Let it sit.

**Pacing note:** This is your exposition-and-tension scene. Don't hurry. Let the players ask Ilse
questions (she'll answer obliquely — she's used to strangers finding the mill strange). The combat
rules come in the next scene; let this one breathe.

---

### Scene 4 — The Man by the River
**What it teaches:** Combat. And the limit of combat.

**Your job:** Split cleanly into two parts:

**Part A — Aldric at the mill-dam.** This is not a combat. This is an atmosphere scene that
happens to have a combat-adjacent choice in it. Describe the Blatant Gift without mechanics first:
something is wrong with the man in the water, and the grogs' bodies know it before their minds
do. Give the players the choice of what to do, then apply mechanics to whatever they choose.

If they approach: the Brave roll (Stress Die, Ease 9). Explain this as a stress roll — there's
something real at stake (their composure, their bodies' own protest). Let the die show them whether
their grog has nerve.

If Albrecht (the Bruiser) goes Berserk and attacks: run it as a full combat tutorial against Aldric's
Parma Magica. This is pedagogically perfect — it teaches all the combat steps *and* then reveals
that the system they just learned doesn't work here. The axe strikes Aldric and... doesn't land.
Doesn't bounce (no visible effect). Simply doesn't connect. Something turns it aside in the last
inch. Three rounds of this and Albrecht's player is frustrated and confused — that's exactly right.

**Part B — The Gate Grog at the mine shafts.** This is the actual combat tutorial. Run it in full:
1. Declare actions around the table.
2. Roll Initiative (Quickness + weapon mod + Stress Die). Write the order on a napkin or your palm.
3. Attacker rolls Attack die. Defender rolls Defense die. Subtract: the difference is Attack Advantage.
4. If Advantage > 0: add it to the weapon's Damage line, subtract the defender's Soak, look up the
   result on the wound range. Apply the wound.
5. Repeat.

Wendel wants them to leave. He will back down if outnumbered and not actively threatened. He will
fight if cornered or if Albrecht charges him before anyone can stop it. Keep the fight short (3–4
rounds) — it's a tutorial, not a pitched battle. Wendel takes a wound, the grogs make their point,
or Wendel holds his ground and they leave. Either way, move to Scene 5.

**If the Storyguide is short on time:** Run only the Aldric encounter, skip the Wendel combat, and
explain combat rules quickly at the end of Scene 4 as a "here's what would have happened" summary.
The magi encounter in Scene 5 is the session's heart — don't be late to it.

**Pacing note:** Scene 4 can take 30–45 minutes if you run a full combat. Budget accordingly.

---

### Scene 5 — The Wave
**What it teaches:** The world is bigger than them.

**Your job:** The key is that Corvus appears not because the grogs found him but because he was
doing something else entirely. He collapsed the shafts because the Speyer filing was rejected and
he needs to remove the legal evidence. The grogs are a secondary concern. They are, in Corvus's
estimation, mildly inconvenient witnesses.

This is the most important distinction: **they are not the threat. They are not worth killing.
They are not worth explaining things to.** Corvus's one sentence — *"You should leave now. I would
prefer not to do this twice."* — is not a villain speech. It's what a man says to geese that have
wandered into his garden before he opens the gate.

Then the wave. Describe it carefully. The earth in this world is not bedrock; it's alive when he
touches it. The swell is smooth and fast and wrong in the same way Aldric was wrong — not violent
exactly, just operating by different rules than the world the grogs thought they were in. Everyone
goes down. Torches out.

The session ends with them on their feet, in the dark, and the tower's lamp burning.

**If players want to fight back in Scene 5:** Let them try. A grog who charges the slope toward
Corvus rolls Initiative and makes it up about fifteen feet before the ground simply doesn't cooperate
anymore — not a second wave, just uneven, listing terrain that costs double movement and rolls a
Quickness + Athletics vs. Ease 9 (Stress) to stay upright. Corvus has already gone. He doesn't
watch them flail. He's not interested.

**The win condition:** They flee. That's it. A session where the grogs run because they're correct
to run is a success. Do not punish the retreat.

**The Scout's scrying-window (optional beat):** If the Scout made her Awareness roll, she sees
Konstantinos's observation window. A circle of too-clear light, hanging in the cold air. When she
looks at it, it vanishes — not like a torch blown out; like a sentence in a book that you look at
directly and the letters rearrange. This is purely atmospheric. Do not explain it. Let it be the
session's last image if the mill-race water isn't.

**Pacing note:** Fifteen minutes, no more. The scene's power is in brevity. The wave, the dark,
the lamp. Get out fast.

## Handling Failure and Grog Death

**Failure is the curriculum.** Every time a roll fails, something in the world becomes clearer —
Dietrich's evasion, Ilse's impossible knowledge, Burkhard's axe not landing. The session is
structured so that failed rolls *advance understanding* rather than blocking progress. A botch can
create a new problem (the confrontation with Ilse, the hostile covenfolk) but it should never
dead-end the session. Always have a path forward, even through the mess.

**Grog death:** It can happen in the combat scenes. If Albrecht goes Berserk and charges into
Wendel's spear with a botched Defense, an Incapacitated result is on the table. The standard Ars
Magica answer is: mourn him fondly and move on. Grogs are expendable; their deaths are memorable;
that's the genre's bargain. Do not fudge it away unless the death would break the session (e.g.,
the Sergeant dying in Scene 2 removes the social anchor the party needs for the rest of the evening).
In that case, a heavy wound that takes him out of Scene 4 is enough — it teaches the same lesson
without ending the show.

**Botches in Scene 5:** Be cautious. *Crest of the Earth Wave* knocks everyone down but deals no
damage — that's deliberate. Corvus is not trying to kill them. A botched Athletics roll to stay
upright means a broken wrist or a cracked rib, not a death. Konstantinos's scrying window has
no physical effect. The climax's power is in the magi's *indifference*, not their lethality.

## Making the Magi Felt Without Showing Them

The three magi are *effects*, not actors, for most of the session. Here's the tool list:

**Konstantinos** appears as:
- Ilse knowing Harbert's name before he introduces himself.
- The mill-keeper's husband mentioning, casually, that "the scholars said we might have visitors."
  He doesn't know why he says "the scholars said." He just knows it.
- The optional scrying-window in Scene 5.
- (If you want a third beat): Somewhere in Rohrbeck, a piece of paper in Dietrich's hall that
  lists the names of all five grogs, written in neat Greek, tucked inside a ledger. The Clerk might
  find it if he looks at the ledger. Konstantinos wrote it three days ago.

**Aldric** appears as:
- The spiraling water in the mill-race.
- The too-even fields.
- Himself, physically present, saying nothing, doing nothing, being wrong in the air around him.
- After he leaves the mill-dam: the Scout notices that the water downstream of where he stood is
  running in perfect straight lines, like someone ruled it.

**Corvus** appears as:
- The sealed mine shafts (his work, done an hour before the grogs arrived).
- The smooth-jointed mill stonework (his work, done two years ago).
- Reeve Dietrich's certainty about the water-rights claim (his work, done four months ago).
- Himself, in moonlight, for thirty seconds.

Together they make a presence that is everywhere and nowhere — the valley feels *managed* from the
start of Scene 2, and the session is the grogs gradually understanding by *whom*, and by what kind
of power, and what that means for them.

**The rule:** the magi are never the answer to "what happens if the grogs do X?" The covenant's
grogs, the covenfolk, the physical environment, and the mundane social web (Dietrich, Ilse) handle
everything up to Scene 5. The magi only enter when the situation is already past resolution.

## Tone and Table Management

**The right tone:** This is not a horror scenario, although the magi are frightening. It is an
*investigation* that discovers it has been an investigation about the wrong thing the whole time.
Werner's questions have correct answers. The grogs are competent. The covenant is not malevolent
toward them. The session should feel like the grogs get smarter and more correct right up until
the moment they understand that "correct" and "safe" are different categories here.

**First-time players:** Reassure them that running away is winning. Say it out loud before Scene 5
if you need to: *"Your job is to bring Werner information. You have it. Getting it home is the game."*

**Players who want to fight:** Let the dice work. Don't fudge Aldric's Parma Magica. Don't soften
the Crest of the Earth Wave. The point is that these are real consequences, not that the Storyguide
is blocking their choices. When Burkhard's axe doesn't land for the third time, his player will
stop asking "can I attack?" and start asking "what are we dealing with?" That question is the
tutorial's graduation.

**Players who are tentative:** The Sergeant and the Clerk are designed for players who want to
engage through conversation and observation. Push the social scenes for them — let Kaspar notice
the deed registration anomaly, let Harbert get a read on Dietrich that nobody else can. Their
contributions are as essential as the combat, and the session's design keeps the investigation
moving whether or not anyone swings a weapon.

**After the session:** Ask the table what they want to know. Why could Ilse do that? What was wrong
with the man in the water? What *was* that wave? These are the questions that turn a tutorial into
a campaign. Take good notes.
