# Character Concept: The Econ-History-Nerd Pinky

> *"Gee, Brain, what do you want to do tonight?"*
> *"The same thing we do every season, Pinky — **try to take over Mythic Europe.**"*

This is the **economic-history-nerd version of Pinky and the Brain**: a magus who, every season,
schemes to take over the world — except the scheme is grounded in *real* 13th-century European
constraints, a *real* medieval worldview, and a *real* ego, and the obstacle isn't a laser fence,
it's the **Order of Hermes and its Code**.

It's also a **classic alt-history protagonist** in the lineage of *A Connecticut Yankee in King
Arthur's Court* and *Dr. Stone*: the mad genius dropped into a low-tech world who sees the
levers nobody else can see and starts pulling them. The difference — and the whole point — is
that our protagonist is **not** a time-traveller with modern values. He's a man *of* 1220 who
happens to have magic, and the drama comes from the collision between what he *could* do and
what a person of his age would actually *want*, *imagine*, and *get away with*.

A third touchstone, for the *faction* he wants to build: the **Brimmed Caps of *Witch Hat
Atelier*** — the wide-brimmed magi who oppose the establishment's restrictions on magic, reject
the idea that the Art should be hidden and fenced in, and use the forbidden freely. They're
villains in that story, but they're exactly the kind of **shadow covenant** our magus would
cultivate: a counter-establishment that denies the legitimacy of the limits it's been handed.
(See §5b.)

It is, unapologetically, a **power fantasy**. The job of the design below is to make it a
*grounded, tension-rich, proactive* power fantasy instead of a spreadsheet.

---

## 1. The core tensions that make this fun

Three constraints pull against each other, and the campaign lives in the friction:

1. **Worldview & ego (verisimilitude):** he must think like a medieval man, not a Civ player.
2. **Proactive play (game design):** he must *do* things, not passively insure against GM-introduced disasters.
3. **Subverting the Order (story engine):** he's set against the Code from the start — confrontation is *inevitable*, and the fun is dancing around the line.

The thesis that reconciles all three: **the magic that is most fun to use, most visibly changes
the world, and most fits a medieval ego is also the magic that most reliably trips the Code.**
We lean *into* that convergence rather than avoiding it.

---

## 2. Worldview & ego — the medieval mind (most important section)

The biggest anachronism risk is making him a benevolent technocrat optimizing aggregate welfare.
A 13th-century person did **not** think that way. Get this right and everything else falls into place.

### What's *absent* from 1220

- **"Progress" as an ideology.** The belief that the world *should* and *can* be systematically
  improved — that the future ought to beat the past — is an Enlightenment invention. The medieval
  temporal sense often ran the *opposite* way: a fallen world declining from Eden toward the
  eschaton. There is no tech tree because nobody believes it exists.
- **The state as an improver.** Nobody targets GDP or public welfare. Prosperity and disaster are
  **Fortune's wheel** and **God's providence/chastisement** — famine is read as divine punishment
  for sin, and the response is penitential (processions, scapegoats, prayer) before it is ever technical.

### What's *present* (and ferocious)

- **Improvement happened — but emergent and interested, never planned and altruistic.** Assarting,
  mills, drainage: real, but driven by population pressure and lordly/monastic *revenue-seeking*.
  The Cistercians drained marshes as a byproduct of *ora et labora* and estate income, not social
  engineering. Don't overcorrect into "callous fatalists," though — there was genuine charity, and
  lords kept peasants alive because peasants were the revenue-generating asset.
- **Ambition in a medieval idiom.** Honor, lineage, lordship, reputation, salvation, autonomy, feud.
  A great man builds a fortress, founds a monastery to buy prayers for his soul, extends his house's
  name, and humiliates his rivals. He maximizes **his standing and his soul's fate within a fixed
  order** — not the size of the pie.

### Translating that to a magus

He's still a man of his time. What he *actually wants*:

- Vis (magical wealth), a great library, apprentices and *filii* to extend his line, a familiar.
- A **covenant as a seat** worthy of him — the magical equivalent of a great hall or a fortress.
- Immortality / longevity, a feared sigil, a voice that carries at Tribunal.

**The locality prospering is a side effect** of building a power base and a monument to himself —
*never* the stated goal. He is not uplifting the peasantry; he is aggrandizing himself, and the
valley happens to get rich in his shadow. (See §4 for how this still produces "societal progress"
in play.)

### Why bite the hand that taught him?

Not ingratitude-as-principle. The medieval frame is **master–apprentice / lord–vassal obligation
that chafes**: the talented junior who has served his time and resents being kept down by a
gerontocracy of elders. Give him a *specific* grievance — a *parens* who slighted him, a rival
House, a Tribunal ruling that went against him — so the ego, not an ideology, sets him against his
teachers. House choice flavors this hard:

- **House Tytalus** — is *supposed* to test authority; conflict is doctrine. The natural fit.
- **House Bonisagus** — ambition dressed as research; "I'm not subverting the Code, I'm advancing the Art."
- **House Flambeau / others** — a pride or grudge motive works for almost anyone.

## 3. The historical context that drives Art & Form choice

This is the research foundation. A 1220 European locality (a manor, village, or covenant's lands;
a few hundred to a few thousand souls) had **two binding constraints, in strict order:
agricultural surplus, then transport.** Almost everything else operates *downstream* and cannot
substitute for them.

### The constraints, ranked by real-world impact

1. **Agricultural surplus — the master constraint.** Yield ratios of ~3–4:1 (vs. 20:1+ today),
   ~85–90% of people farming to feed everyone, and recurrent famine. The killer was **variance**,
   not just low averages — one bad year meant hunger, two in a row meant mass death (cf. the Great
   Famine of 1315–17). Everything — towns, specialists, soldiers, cathedrals — sits on the thin
   surplus above subsistence. **Reliable abundance is the single most transformative thing magic
   can deliver**, a bigger jump than the entire High Medieval agricultural revolution, concentrated
   in one valley. (Don't neglect the **livestock** half: oxen were irreplaceable capital; murrain
   could ruin a community — cf. the cattle panzootic of 1319–20.)
2. **Land reclamation & hydraulic engineering — the underrated #2.** Medieval wealth-creation was
   mostly *draining marshes, clearing forest, and irrigating* — the Cistercian/Dutch playbook.
   Every reclaimed acre is *new* productive base. A tireless magical labor corps = a monastic
   engineering order at zero cost.
3. **Productive capital — mills, not castles.** The defining capital good was the **watermill**
   (~6,000 in Domesday England, 1086), a labor multiplier lords monopolized as cash cows. Cheap
   magical construction is radical *only when aimed at productive capital* (mill-races, dikes,
   terraces, bridges), not at impressive-but-inert status architecture.
4. **Transport — the great invisible tax.** Overland grain roughly *doubled* in price every
   50–100 miles by cart; water transport cost ~1/10–1/20 as much. Collapsing transport cost turns
   a landlocked surplus into *sellable* wealth and enables specialization — but it's strictly
   second-order: worthless until production is solved.

### The "sounds great, isn't" pile (so we don't waste a build on them)

- **Conjured gold/gems:** causes inflation without real wealth (the Spanish price revolution),
  and is the most conspicuous possible act. Discreet liquidity at best, not a foundation.
- **Weather control:** feels godlike, behaves chaotically; the variance-reduction you actually want
  is delivered more cleanly by protecting crops directly. And nothing screams *witchcraft* like a
  valley with private weather.
- **Healthcare:** emotionally huge, demographically double-edged (lowering mortality without more
  food just presses harder on the Malthusian ceiling). Real value is *narrow and high* — keep your
  irreplaceable specialists and rulers alive — not aggregate productivity.
- **Communication / scrying / lie-detection:** superb tools of *statecraft and control*, not
  *production*. They grow your reach and grip once you're already big.
- **Defense (Aegis, walls):** protects the surplus, doesn't generate it. Necessary, not generative.
- **Mining/prospecting:** the one mid-tier item that punches up — knowing exactly where the
  **silver** is cuts the brutal cost/risk of prospecting (cf. Freiberg, Kutná Hora boomtowns).

## 4. Game-design pillar: proactive over reactive

This is the lens that overrides "historically optimal" when they conflict.

- **Reactive / insurance magic** (Aegis, wards, healing, famine- and murrain-proofing) only *means*
  anything if the GM actively runs the disaster it negates, and its benefit is **invisible** —
  "your valley is quietly 30% richer in the background" is a spreadsheet, not a scene. It relies on
  the GM to introduce the very problems it solves, or to model background impact — difficult and
  un-fun. **Dramatically inert.**
- **Proactive / world-changing magic** (construction, reclamation, terraforming, transport,
  prospecting, scry-then-act, influence) lets the *player initiate*, changes the map *visibly*, and
  spools out consequences *without* the GM pre-loading a threat. This is where agency and story live.

**Design rule: favor spells that let the player DO something that visibly changes the world and
generates consequences, over spells that quietly negate hypothetical bad outcomes.**

### The test (for other agents evaluating a spell)

Ask one question: **"Does this spell's value depend on the GM *first* introducing the problem it
solves?"** If yes, it's reactive and at risk of sitting dead in play, *no matter how cool it is*.
The danger is not that these spells are weak — it's that they're **cool on paper and silent at the
table**, because their entire payoff is contingent on a scenario the GM has to remember to stage.

### Worked examples — genuinely cool, genuinely at risk

- **Watching Ward** (store a devastating spell that fires the instant you're killed). Fantastic,
  flavorful, *the* dramatic wizard move. But it does **literally nothing** unless the GM kills you
  under the exact triggering conditions — it can sit dormant for the entire campaign and never once
  resolve on screen. Pure reactive jeopardy-bait.
- **Aegis of the Hearth** (the great covenant ward). Iconic and mandatory in fiction — and yet in
  *play* it's an invisible background stat that only "happens" on the rare session the GM throws an
  outside attacker at it. The player rarely gets to *do* anything with it.
- **Frosty Breath of the Spoken Lie** (infallible lie detector). Sounds like a power move. In
  practice it needs the GM to (a) introduce a liar, (b) make the lie matter, and (c) not just narrate
  the deception away — and needs the player to suspect and deploy it at the right beat. Most sessions
  it never fires, or the GM tells you someone's lying anyway.
- **Famine- / murrain-proofing** (e.g. crop-protection rituals). Historically the *most powerful*
  thing on the whole list (see §3) — but its benefit is a number that only exists if the GM runs the
  famine it prevents. Otherwise the player feels nothing. "Your valley quietly didn't starve" is not
  a scene.
- **Ward against [faeries / beasts / wood / etc.]**, healing spells, most defensive abjurations:
  same shape — value = (probability the GM sends the matching threat) × (how lethal it would've been).

### The contrast — same coolness, self-activating

The proactive equivalents carry their own activation energy; the player triggers the payoff:

- **Terraforming / construction** — *you* decide to drain the marsh or raise the hold; the changed
  map persists and spawns new situations whether or not the GM planned them.
- **Hermes' Portal** — *you* build the network and then run schemes *through* it.
- **Miner's Keen Eye** — *you* go find the silver; the strike spawns a boomtown, a greedy lord, a
  covetous bishop — plot the player authored.
- **Aura of Rightful Authority** — *you* go bend the baron *this scene*, now, on purpose.

### Rescuing a reactive spell

A reactive pick isn't banned — it's redeemed by **making its contingency the player's choice and
making the result visible** (the §5 "visibility = tension" move). Famine-proofing becomes proactive
the moment the *player* engineers the comparison — feasting their own folk in a hunger year *as a
deliberate, conspicuous flex* that draws the neighbors' priest. The spell didn't change; the player
seized the trigger instead of waiting for the GM to pull it.

Note the synthesis with §2: even the historically-supreme food magic only becomes good *theater*
when made **visible and comparative** — the year *our* folk feast while the next valley starves,
and that valley's lord and priest come asking how. Visibility = tension. So we make even our
"insurance" proactive and conspicuous by choice.

### How this delivers "societal progress" without anachronism

The PC pursues *personal* aggrandizement (a seat, a power base, a monument, revenge on a rival).
The **societal progress is the emergent, visible byproduct** the players get to author through
proactive acts — a drained marsh becomes a demesne, a silver strike becomes a boomtown, a portal
becomes a trade network. The *player* experiences the alt-history "I'm bootstrapping a civilization"
power fantasy; the *character* experiences "I am becoming the greatest power in the Tribunal." Both
are true at once, and that gap is the show.

## 5. The Order tension — the story engine

"Interfering with mundanes" is the **elastic, case-law clause** argued at *every* Tribunal. The
proactive, visible, fun-to-use magic — reshaping land, raising a hold, opening a mine, getting a
reeve to obey, making a valley conspicuously blessed — is *precisely* what draws mundane and Church
attention and drags you before a Quaesitor.

**The escalation ladder ("will they rule for me *this* time?"):**

1. **Deniable.** A little reshaped land, a healed steward. Nobody cares yet.
2. **Noticed.** A rival magus or slighted neighbor files at Tribunal. First jeopardy beat — he wins
   on a technicality, a patron's favor, or a clever reading of "interference."
3. **Bolder, split decision.** Clearly near the line; the Tribunal divides; he wins but now *owes
   favors* and has made enemies who remember.
4. **Existential.** A real (or framed) breach; the Church is now looking at the *Order* because of
   him. **Wizard's March** looms. Campaign climax.

Because the Peripheral Code is case-law argued at a *political* Tribunal, every escalation is
genuinely arguable — the ruling turns on precedent, allies, House clout, and how well the players
argue. The "dancing around the line" engine is **native to the setting**, not bolted on.

### The real game: laundering abundance

The drama is never the spell — it's the **cover story**. A valley that never has a bad harvest is a
*theological* problem before it's a Hermetic one. The recurring challenge is laundering conspicuous,
structural abundance into something that looks like ordinary good management, good fortune, and
God's favor — explaining to a Quaesitor why the demesne is suspiciously blessed *without* admitting
he's quietly become a petty lord under the Order's nose.

## 5b. The shadow covenant — denying the limits, not just the law

The escalation ladder above is about *getting caught*. This section is about *what he's actually
building toward*, and it's the **Brimmed Caps** model from *Witch Hat Atelier*: not a lone
law-breaker but a **counter-establishment** — a shadow covenant of like-minded magi who reject the
premises the Order hands down, gather the talented and the disaffected, and treat the rules as
arbitrary fences rather than natural law.

There are three escalating *kinds* of pushback he can mount, and a great campaign probably climbs
through all of them:

1. **Defy the Code in practice.** Do the forbidden thing and survive the Tribunal — the §5 ladder.
   This is *behavioral* rebellion.
2. **Attack a specific clause as illegitimate.** Pick one provision and argue it shouldn't exist —
   that "interfering with mundanes" is a cowardly leash invented by elders to keep juniors small,
   or that the prohibition is selectively enforced by whoever holds the votes. This is *political /
   ideological* rebellion: he's not hiding from the Code, he's **litigating its right to bind him**,
   building precedent and allies at Tribunal until the clause itself frays. (This is the most
   on-genre with the Brimmed Caps — open opposition, a manifesto, a following.)
3. **Deny the Limits of Magic themselves.** The juiciest and most "mad genius." Hermetic theory is
   bounded by stated **Limits** (Vis, Energy, Creation, the Soul, the Divine, the Infernal, Time,
   Arcane Connections, the Lunar Sphere, Essential Nature, Aging, etc.). The orthodox position is
   that these are *natural law*. Our magus's heresy: **some of those "limits" are merely the edge of
   what Bonisagus happened to formalize — failures of imagination dressed up as cosmology.** He
   doesn't accept "magic cannot do X"; he asks *which limit, and says who?* This is **literally how
   the Order's own magic was built and still advances** — Original Research, Hermetic Breakthroughs,
   and the Integration of non-Hermetic traditions — so it's defensible *and* radical at once, and it
   gives the mad-genius power fantasy a real in-fiction engine: he's not just casting big spells,
   he's **expanding the boundary of the possible** and daring the Order to call it impossible.

The throughline: the Order taught him the Art *and* the cage, and he denies the cage is real — first
in deed, then in argument, then in metaphysics. The shadow covenant is where he gathers the people,
the vis, and the research to make the denial stick. That progression — outlaw → dissident →
heresiarch of magical theory — is the spine of the long campaign.

## 6. What this implies for Art & Form choice

Detailed spell selection is deferred (and gated by a young magus only reliably handling ~15–25 in
one Art). The *direction* is set by the sections above:

- **Prime candidates: Terram & Herbam.** They are the **proactive, map-changing, productive-capital
  and reclamation** Arts — earth, stone, building, drainage, terracing; wood, crops, living walls,
  the agricultural base. They directly serve the top two real constraints (surplus, reclamation)
  *and* the medieval-ego "build me a seat" idiom *and* the proactive-play rule. They also generate
  the most visible, Code-tickling consequences. This is the spine.
- **Rego as the anchor Technique** is attractive: it concentrates deep low-level utility (labor,
  movement, control, and eventually *Hermes' Portal* for the late-game network), so one maxed verb
  fans out across several Forms.
- **Wildly open to surprises — and the non-obvious answer is the *better* answer.** Terram/Herbam
  are the obvious heart, but obvious is the least interesting outcome. If the build maths out toward
  an **Intellego** "intelligence-state" magus (prospecting → boomtowns, scry-then-act statecraft), a
  **Vim** meta-mage (the infrastructure that powers and protects everything else, plus the
  Breakthrough research from §5b), or some genuinely lateral combination nobody would predict, **that
  is a *win*, not a compromise.** A magus who takes over the world with *Aquam* and *Mentem*, or who
  routes an entire economy through one weird *Imaginem* trick, is more fun, more memorable, and more
  in keeping with the mad-genius / Brimmed-Cap spirit than the predictable earth-and-wood engineer.
  The whole pitch is *seeing levers nobody else sees* — so a clever, unexpected, "wait, you can do
  **that** with it?" build is the dream, not a fallback. Reward lateral thinking; treat the obvious
  pick as the floor to beat.

**Selection filter for any candidate spell/Art:** Does it (1) let the player *act* and visibly change
the world, (2) read as medieval ego rather than modern uplift, and (3) plausibly attract Code
scrutiny? Three yeses = on-concept — *regardless of how unconventional the Art is.* Surprising routes
to those three yeses are explicitly prized.

## 7. Open questions / next steps

- **House & specific grievance:** Tytalus (doctrinal conflict) vs. ambitious Bonisagus vs. a
  grudge-driven other — and the precise slight that sets him against his elders.
- **The heresy (§5b):** which *clause* he litigates against, and which *Limit of Magic* he refuses
  to accept as real — the specific boundary his career is dedicated to breaking.
- **The shadow covenant:** who the first Brimmed-Cap-style recruits are (the disaffected, the
  talented, the marched-adjacent), and how he gathers them without the gathering itself tipping off
  the Quaesitors.
- **The seat:** what locality, what mundane entanglement (a lord, a bishop, a town) seeds the first
  Tribunal complaints.
- **Opening build:** Technique/Form scores, a gauntlet-budget spell list, and supporting Abilities
  (Magic Theory, Finesse, Penetration, Concentration) — to be pulled from `data/spells.db`.
- **The first proactive act** the campaign opens on (drain the marsh? find the silver? raise the
  tower?) — the inciting "Brain unveils tonight's scheme" beat.
